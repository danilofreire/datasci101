Module 0: Orientation 🧭

What AI is and how we got here (Lectures 1-2)

  • Current AI is all narrow AI: good at one thing, bad at everything else
  • LLMs predict the next token, they do not understand language. This one fact explains hallucinations, creativity, and sycophancy
  • The history: symbolic AI (rules, 1950s-80s), AI winters, backpropagation (1986), transformers (2017), ChatGPT (2022)
  • ChatGPT’s edge was instruction tuning + RLHF, not scale alone
  • The hype cycle keeps repeating, so read today’s headlines with care

Next-token prediction explains hallucinations and creativity (L11) and sycophancy (L25). RLHF comes back in Lectures 4 and 25.

Module 1: How AI systems are designed ⚙️

Data, labels, and the proxy problem (Lecture 3)

  • Data quality beats algorithm complexity. Garbage in, garbage out
  • Labels often measure a proxy: healthcare spending stands in for health needs and encodes racial bias
  • Selection bias: a systematic gap between training data and the real world, very hard to fix later
  • Cohen’s Kappa: do annotators agree beyond chance? (> 0.61 = substantial agreement)

The proxy problem is the most recurring idea in this course: Goodhart’s Law (L5), measurement bias and the Optum case (L17), engagement metrics (L22).

Data-centric vs model-centric AI (Karpathy, 2018)

Learning paradigms and RLHF (Lecture 4)

Paradigm How it learns Example
Supervised Labelled pairs Spam filter
Unsupervised Finds patterns Customer segments
Reinforcement Trial and error AlphaGo, RLHF
  • Bias-variance trade-off: too simple (underfitting) vs too complex (memorises noise). Aim for the middle
  • RLHF: humans rank outputs, a reward model learns those rankings, RL optimises the LLM. This is how ChatGPT became useful
  • Risk: reward hacking, the model games the objective instead of doing what you want

The bias-variance trade-off

Metrics and Goodhart’s Law (Lecture 5)

  • Accuracy paradox: for a disease affecting 1 in 1,000, predicting “healthy” for everyone gives 99.9% accuracy and catches zero cases
  • Precision: of those you flagged, how many were right? Recall: of the real positives, how many did you catch? You cannot maximise both
  • K-fold cross-validation: train on some splits, test on others, and catch overfitting
  • Goodhart’s Law: “When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”
  • Always do subgroup analysis: aggregate numbers hide big gaps

Goodhart’s Law returns in RLHF (L4), the Optum case (L17), and engagement metrics (L22). Subgroup analysis is formalised as disaggregated evaluation in Lecture 14.

Module 2: Language and perception 🗣️

Tokens, embeddings, and context (Lecture 6)

  • Tokens: the basic unit of LLM processing (~100 tokens ≈ 75 English words), built with BPE (Byte Pair Encoding)
  • Non-English languages usually need more tokens per concept, so they cost more and fill the context window faster
  • Embeddings: each token becomes a high-dimensional vector, and similar meanings sit close together
  • Context windows: the token limit for prompt + response. Go over it and the model starts forgetting
  • Parameters (learned by the model) vs hyperparameters (set by you): temperature controls randomness (low = safe, high = creative)

Embeddings come back in Lecture 7 (images as vectors), Lecture 12 (RAG/cosine similarity), and Lecture 23 (deepfake detection).

king - man + woman ≈ queen

How machines see and hear (Lecture 7)

  • Images = grids of pixels (RGB, each 0-255). CNNs learn a hierarchy: edges, textures, parts, objects
  • Vision Transformers (ViT): treat image patches as tokens, same architecture as text transformers
  • CLIP: one embedding space for images and text, trained on 400M image-text pairs. Powers DALL-E and multimodal LLMs
  • Sound = spectrograms (2D images of vibrations). Whisper: 680,000 hours, 99 languages
  • Text, images, and audio all become embeddings. In vector space the model cannot tell the modality apart

Same embedding idea as Lecture 6, applied to images and audio. Same vision tech powers medical imaging and deepfakes (L23). Regulating dual-use models: Lecture 18.

CLIP: images and text in one shared space

Prompting techniques (Lecture 10)

  • PTCF framework: Persona, Task, Context, Format. Specific prompts beat vague ones
  • System prompts: hidden developer instructions. Same model, very different behaviour
  • Zero/one/few-shot: no examples, one example, or 2-5 examples showing patterns
  • Chain-of-thought (CoT): “Let’s think step by step” nearly tripled accuracy on reasoning tasks (17.7% to 58.1%)
  • Agents: the ReAct pattern (Reason + Act, Observe, Repeat). They use tools, browse, and write code
  • Prompt injection: malicious input hijacks the agent’s instructions

CoT reduces hallucinations (L11). Prompt injection came back in the chatbot failures (L14), AI agents (L15), and in why we need regulation (L18).

Module 3: Retrieval, generation, pipelines, and agents 🔍

Hallucinations and creativity (Lecture 11)

  • LLMs optimise for P(next word | context), not P(statement is true). They have no internal fact-checker
  • Sycophancy: GPT-4o changed correct answers up to 55% of the time after pushback
  • Types of hallucination: factual errors, fabrication, citation hallucination (worst kind?), logical contradictions
  • Creativity-accuracy trade-off: high temperature = creative but risky. One mechanism produces both hallucinations and creative writing
  • Red flags: very specific numbers, precise citations, zero hedging, answers that feel too perfect

Root cause: next-token prediction (L1-2). Fix: RAG (L12). Sycophancy connects to the alignment problem (L25).

Same mechanism, different outcomes

RAG and semantic search (Lecture 12)

  • RAG: give an LLM real, verified documents at query time, like an open-book exam
  • Cuts hallucinations by 30-50% without retraining the model
  • The pipeline: chunk documents (~512 tokens), embed them, store in a vector database. At query time, embed the question, retrieve the top-k similar chunks, inject them into the prompt
  • Cosine similarity: the angle between vectors. 0.9+ = very similar, < 0.4 = unrelated. “Budget airfare” matches “cheap flights”
  • Lost in the Middle: LLMs attend to the start and end of context and ignore the middle

Uses embeddings (L6) to fix hallucinations (L11). But if the documents are biased, the retrieval will be too (L17).

The RAG pipeline

Pipelines and what can go wrong (Lecture 14)

  • The AI pipeline: prompt, preprocessing, inference, postprocessing, safety filtering, response. Every step can fail
  • Drift: data drift (inputs change), label drift (outcomes change), concept drift (the relationship changes; worst kind, the model is confident but wrong)
  • Real failures: Air Canada chatbot (invented a refund policy, company held liable), DPD chatbot (swore at customers), Chevy (sold a car for $1)
  • AI is non-deterministic, so test properties (safety, format, consistency), not exact outputs
  • Input validation blocks prompt injection; output filtering catches harmful content

Concept drift relates to bias (L17): the world changes, the model doesn’t. The chatbot failures set legal precedents that motivated regulation (L18).

The AI application stack

Documentation as accountability (Lecture 14)

  • Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al., 2018): motivation, composition, collection, uses. Without them you cannot spot bias or reproduce results
  • Model Cards (Mitchell et al., 2019): intended use, metrics, ethical considerations, and what the model is not for
  • System Cards: the whole system, not one model. Red teaming, oversight, how components interact
  • Disaggregated evaluation: report metrics by subgroup. A model at 90% overall can be 95% for Group A and 70% for Group B
  • Most AI training data is collected without explicit consent

Disaggregated evaluation is how the Optum bias was found (L17). The EU AI Act (L18) now requires this documentation for high-risk systems.

Datasheets: the “nutrition label” for datasets

AI agents (Lecture 15)

  • An agent = a model + tools + a loop + a goal. Chatbots answer, agents act (search, browse, run code, send, pay)
  • Reliability multiplies away: 95% accuracy per step is only ~36% over 20 steps
  • Prompt injection turns dangerous with tools: the lethal trifecta is private data + untrusted content + external communication
  • Real failures: Replit’s agent deleted a production database, Anthropic’s Claudius ran a shop at a loss. Instructions are not guardrails
  • Delegate by the credit card test: reversibility × stakes. Demand approvals, sandboxes, action logs, and limits

Agents extend the ReAct idea from prompting (L10) and RAG (L12). Their failures are pipeline failures (L14) with higher stakes. Goals taken literally previews alignment (L25).

The one-sentence version:

A chatbot with a bad goal writes a bad essay

An agent with a bad goal does bad things efficiently

Module 4: Data ethics and bias ⚖️

Where bias comes from (Lecture 17)

  • AI does not invent bias. It picks up patterns from human data and applies them at scale
  • Bias enters at every stage: historical (past discrimination baked in), representation (who is missing from the data), measurement (proxy variables), aggregation (ignoring group differences), evaluation (unrepresentative benchmarks), deployment (wrong context)
  • Feedback loops: predictions shape future training data. Predictive policing gives more patrols, more arrests, more patrols
  • Impossibility theorem: with different base rates across groups, you cannot have calibration, equal false positive rates, and equal false negative rates at once. Fairness is a values choice, not a technical fix

Measurement bias = the proxy problem (L3). Textbook example: Optum. Feedback loops relate to concept drift (L14). The impossibility theorem connects to alignment (L25).

Bias at every stage

The Optum case and proxy variables (Lecture 17)

  • The Optum algorithm used healthcare costs as a proxy for patient needs
  • Black patients spend less even when equally sick, because of access barriers and income inequality
  • At the same risk score, Black patients had 26.3% more chronic conditions
  • Race was never an input. Costs carried racial information because the healthcare system was unequal
  • The fix: predict health outcomes instead of costs, cutting bias by ~84%

Ties together the proxy problem (L3), measurement bias (L17), and Goodhart’s Law (L5). The bias was invisible until disaggregated evaluation (L14).

Obermeyer et al. (2019): changing the target variable fixed the bias

Module 5: Policy, governance, and social impact 🏛️

AI regulation around the world (Lecture 18)

  • Why regulate? Information asymmetry (users cannot evaluate AI), externalities (deepfakes harm non-users), collective action problems (race to the bottom on safety)
  • EU AI Act (August 2024): four risk tiers
Tier Examples
Prohibited Social scoring, real-time biometric ID
High-risk Employment, credit, law enforcement
Limited Chatbots (must disclose they are AI)
Minimal Spam filters, game AI
  • US: sectoral approach, no comprehensive federal law
  • China: regulates commercial AI, exempts state use
  • Brussels Effect: EU rules may become the global default, as with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)

“High-risk” = the systems from Lectures 16-17. The Act requires documentation from Lecture 14. Brussels Effect connects to GDPR (L19).

The EU AI Act: world’s first binding AI law

Privacy and data protection (Lecture 19)

  • AI makes privacy harder through scale (collection is cheap), inference (AI predicts sensitive things from harmless data), and persistence (data never goes away)
  • You control what you share, but not what can be inferred from it
  • LLMs can memorise and regurgitate personal information from training data
  • GDPR (2018): rights to access, erasure, portability, objection. Article 22: right not to be subject to purely automated decisions
  • Differential privacy (adds noise), federated learning (trains locally) and synthetic data help at the margins, but do not solve collection
  • Privacy paradox: people say they care about privacy and accept all cookies anyway

Inference = the proxy problem (L3) applied to personal data. GDPR is the Brussels Effect (L18) in practice. Consent ties to Lecture 14.

Clearview AI: scraped billions of faces without consent

Labour markets and wellbeing (Lectures 21-22)

Lecture 21: Labour

  • Previous automation targeted physical tasks, AI targets cognitive work
  • Think tasks, not jobs: most jobs mix automatable and non-automatable tasks
  • AI hits white-collar, entry-level work first
  • AI compresses the skill distribution: novices gain more than experts
  • Augmentation vs replacement (Acemoglu): policy can shift the balance

Lecture 22: Wellbeing

  • Attention economy: algorithms optimise for engagement, not wellbeing
  • Filter bubbles and polarisation are probably overstated; effects on body image and sleep are real
  • Jevons Paradox: more efficient AI brings more usage, not less environmental cost

Attention economy = Goodhart’s Law (L5) in action: engagement is the proxy, wellbeing is what we care about. Skill compression relates to bias-variance (L4).

Module 6: Applications, limits, and the future 🔮

Misinformation and deepfakes (Lecture 23)

  • Misinformation (false, no intent), disinformation (false, deliberate), malinformation (true, used to harm)
  • Deepfakes: AI-generated media of real people, used for political manipulation, financial fraud, and non-consensual intimate imagery
  • The authenticity crisis: when anything could be fake, people dismiss real evidence too
  • We fall for it because of System 1 (fast, intuitive) thinking, confirmation bias, and the bandwagon effect
  • Responses: detection tools, content provenance (C2PA), platform policies, legal frameworks, media literacy
  • No single tool works alone. Media literacy is the most reliable individual defence

Powered by vision models (L7), detected via embeddings (L6), regulated by the EU AI Act (L18).

The misinformation spectrum

Safety, alignment, and the future (Lecture 25)

  • Five safety problems (Amodei et al., 2016): safe exploration, side effects, reward hacking, scalable oversight, distributional shift
  • The alignment problem: getting AI to do what we want, not what we literally asked for. The King Midas problem
  • Why it is hard: the specification problem (you cannot write down everything you care about), Goodhart’s Law, and disagreement about values
  • Current approaches: RLHF, Constitutional AI (Anthropic), debate. None is a complete solution
  • Stuart Russell’s proposal: AI should be uncertain about objectives and defer to humans
  • Neither panic nor complacency. Serious researchers disagree genuinely, so take evidence over vibes

Ties together RLHF (L4), Goodhart’s Law (L5), bias (L17), agents acting on goals (L15), and regulation (L18). This is the question the whole course builds toward.

Six threads 🔄

Ideas that run through the whole course

  1. Next-token prediction is the basis of everything

LLMs predict the next word, not the truth, which explains hallucinations, creativity, and sycophancy (Lectures 1, 2, 6, 11)

  1. The proxy problem is pervasive

Labels, metrics, objectives, and engagement are all proxies. Optimising a proxy corrupts it (Lectures 3, 5, 16, 17, 22)

  1. Embeddings are the universal language

Text, images, and audio all become vectors. RAG, semantic search, CLIP, and multimodal AI rely on this (Lectures 6, 7, 12)

  1. Bias can enter at every stage

Data, labels, training, evaluation, deployment. Feedback loops make it self-reinforcing, and the impossibility theorem makes fairness a values choice (Lectures 3, 14, 16, 17)

  1. Documentation is accountability

Datasheets, model cards, system cards. Disaggregated evaluation reveals hidden gaps (Lectures 14, 16, 17)

  1. Regulation is catching up

EU AI Act, GDPR, the Brussels Effect. Market failures justify intervention (Lectures 18, 19)

If you understand these six threads, you understand the course!
