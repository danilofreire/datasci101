Privacy in the AI age

Why AI makes privacy harder

Older privacy laws did not anticipate these problems:

Scale of collection:

  • Unlike staffed surveillance, AI runs 24/7, almost for free
  • Every click, scroll and location ping becomes data

Inference capabilities:

  • AI predicts sensitive traits from harmless data
  • Shopping → health, typing → mood, friends → politics
  • It infers what you never disclosed

Persistence:

  • Data doesn’t decay: today’s models affect you forever
  • Decisions follow you across contexts
  • “Right to be forgotten” is technically hard

Source: AI Multiple

“If you’re not paying for the product, you are the product.” Even paying customers are often the product

What can be inferred from your data?

Research shows AI can predict:

Data source Inferred
Facebook likes Political views, sexuality, personality

Typing patterns Age, gender, emotional state
Purchase history Pregnancy, health conditions

Example: Target and pregnancy

  • Target mailed baby coupons to a pregnant teen
  • Her father complained: she was “still in high school”
  • Days later, he apologised: she was pregnant
  • The algorithm knew before her family did (NYT)

Source: Time

You can control what you share, but you cannot control what can be inferred from it

Training data and privacy

The training data problem:

  • Web-scale data includes personal information
  • Models can memorise and regurgitate training data
  • Your name, address or phone number may be in there

Demonstrated attacks:

  • Carlini et al. (2021) pulled verbatim data from GPT-2
  • Leaked: names, phone numbers, emails
  • Extraction attacks keep improving

The consent problem:

  • Most people don’t know what’s in training sets
  • “Publicly available” ≠ “consented to AI training”

  • We’re bad at judging risk

Legal frameworks

GDPR fundamentals

The General Data Protection Regulation (2016/679, in force since 2018) is the EU’s comprehensive privacy law

Main principles:

  1. Lawfulness, fairness, transparency
  2. Purpose limitation: use data only for the stated purpose
  3. Data minimisation: collect only what’s necessary
  4. Accuracy: keep data correct
  5. Storage limitation: delete it when no longer needed
  6. Integrity and confidentiality: protect data properly
  7. Accountability: demonstrate compliance

Six lawful bases: consent, contract, legal obligation, vital interests, public task and legitimate interests

  • Legitimate interests is the most contested basis
  • Firms use it to skip consent, even for AI training

Like the EU AI Act, GDPR covers anyone processing EU residents’ data, wherever they are. A US startup with EU users must comply

Individual rights under GDPR

You have the right to:

Right What it means
Access Get a copy of your data
Rectification Correct inaccurate data
Erasure “Right to be forgotten”
Portability Move data to another service
Restriction Limit processing
Object Stop certain processing
Automated decisions Ask for a human in significant ones

Article 22: automated decisions

  • Right not to be subject to purely automated decisions
  • Consequential decisions need human involvement
  • Exceptions: contracts and explicit consent

Source: GDPR.eu (Proton)

In practice, these rights are hard to use: firms hide forms, reply slowly or claim exemptions

GDPR and AI tensions

  1. Purpose limitation vs model training
    • Can data given for X train a model for Y?
    • AI companies claim legitimate interest
  2. Data minimisation vs big data
    • AI wants more data than GDPR allows
    • What’s “necessary” for an LLM? Everything?
  3. Right to explanation vs black boxes
    • You can ask why an AI decided about you
    • Many AI systems can’t explain themselves
  4. Right to erasure vs model training
    • Can you demand removal from a trained model?
    • “Unlearning” is technically hard

Source: CertPro

GDPR predates LLMs, so applying 2018 law to 2026 technology raises open questions

US privacy landscape

No comprehensive federal law, just a patchwork of sector rules:

Law Scope Protects
HIPAA Healthcare data Patient medical records
FERPA Education records Student academic data
COPPA Children’s data Under-13 online activity
GLBA Financial data Bank and loan records
FCRA Credit reporting Credit scores and history
ECPA Electronic comms Emails, calls, stored data

States fill the gap:

  • California (CCPA/CPRA): closest to GDPR
  • Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut: similar frameworks
  • Illinois BIPA: individuals can sue over biometric data
  • 23 states have such laws (Vermont joined June 2026)

Technical approaches to privacy

Differential privacy

Differential privacy adds random noise to results, so no one can tell if you are in the data (Dwork & Roth, 2014)

  • “How many patients have diabetes?” True answer: 137
  • Released: 134 or 141, close enough for research
  • Patterns stay visible, but individuals stay hidden

Epsilon (ε) is set by the data owner before release:

  • ε limits how much one person can change a result
  • Small ε (below 1): more noise, strong privacy
  • Large ε (around 10): less noise, weak privacy
  • Small ε: no one can tell if the count is 136 or 137

Source: Flower AI

Used by: Apple (emoji suggestions), Google (Chrome stats), US Census (2020, ε = 19.61)

Federated learning

Federated learning trains models without pooling the data (McMahan et al., 2017)

How it works:

  1. A server sends the same model to many devices
  2. Each device trains it on its local data
  3. Each returns only what it learned (weights)
  4. The server averages updates and repeats

Privacy gains and limits:

  • Raw data never leaves your device
  • Updates can still leak information
  • Coordinating thousands of devices is hard

Source: Wikipedia

Example: Google’s phone keyboard (Gboard) learns from your typing, but your texts stay on your phone

Synthetic data for privacy

Synthetic data looks real but isn’t (Jordon et al., 2022)

How it works:

  • Train a generative model on real data
  • Generate new, fake records
  • Train your AI on the fakes

Benefits and limits:

  • Fewer direct links to real people
  • But generators can still leak training records
  • Easier to share for research
  • May miss rare cases

Source: GOV.UK

Used by: health researchers (fake patient records), banks (fraud detection tests)

Do these techniques actually help?

What they can do:

  • Allow research on sensitive health data
  • Give engineers concrete tools
  • Differential privacy is provable
  • Federated learning keeps data on the device

What they cannot do:

  • Stop data collection in the first place
  • Fix the power imbalance with platforms
  • Make users understand data use

However:

  • The best protection is not collecting data
  • AI firms’ logic: more data = more profit
  • Technical fixes work within the system, not on it

Beware “privacy washing”: differential privacy with large ε (weak privacy), or federated learning that still collects metadata

Challenges and tensions

Case study: Clearview AI

  • Jan 2020: the NYT exposed 3 billion scraped photos
  • Clearview now has over 70 billion social media photos
  • It built a face search engine without consent or notice
  • Sold to thousands of US police agencies
  • Police ran ~1M searches by 2023

Legal fallout:

  • The ACLU sued under Illinois BIPA (2020)
  • 2022 settlement bans sales to most private firms
  • France (CNIL) and Italy: €20M fine each
  • Netherlands: €30.5M fine (2024)
  • Australia, Canada: ordered to delete data
  • UK: still in court

Source: Library of Congress

Sold as police-only, but accounts went to retailers, banks and investors’ friends. Critics call it a “perpetual police line-up”

  • Your location and activity

In exchange, it provides:

  2. What would make you change your mind?
  3. What data would be “too much”?
  4. Does it matter if a firm, hospital or state runs it?

Points to raise

What can you do?

Individual actions:

  • Review your privacy settings
  • Use privacy tools (Signal, DuckDuckGo, Firefox)
  • Limit location sharing
  • Use a different email for each service
  • Use your GDPR/CCPA rights (access, deletion)
  • Be sceptical of “personalisation”

Limits of individual action:

  • Opting out often means losing the service
  • Privacy is collective: others’ data reveals yours
  • The power imbalance is structural

Collective action:

  • Back privacy laws
  • Demand transparency from companies
  • Support EFF, EPIC or noyb
  • Choose privacy-respecting services
  • Vote for pro-privacy candidates

Signal over WhatsApp helps, but only legislation changes how insurers use your health data

Summary

Main takeaways

AI and privacy

  • Collection at unprecedented scale
  • Inference makes “non-sensitive” data sensitive
  • Models can memorise and leak personal data

Legal frameworks

  • GDPR: comprehensive and rights-based
  • US: a sectoral patchwork
  • AI development and data protection pull against each other

Technical approaches

  • Noise, decentralised training, artificial data
  • All have trade-offs

Key insights

  • Collection is the core problem
  • The same data enables benefits and surveillance
  • Privacy is collective, not just individual
  • Technical fixes don’t address power imbalances
  • Rules and oversight decide whether data helps or harms

Appendix

Quick quiz: possible answers

1. Messages used to train AI

  • Breaks transparency and purpose limitation
  • Fix: tell users and ask for opt-in consent

2. Location every 30 seconds

  • Breaks data minimisation
  • Fix: track only while the app is open
  • Use approximate location where possible

3. Data kept “just in case”

  • Breaks storage limitation
  • Old data adds breach risk for no benefit
  • Fix: set retention periods, then delete

4. Hiring tool with no human review

  • Breaks GDPR Article 22 on automated decisions
  • Fix: a human reviews every rejection
  • Candidates can ask why and contest it

Where’s your line: points to raise

1. Would you use it?

  • The app is free, so your data likely pays for it
  • Insurer discounts can turn into higher premiums

2. What would change your mind?

  • Data stays on your phone
  • No sale to third parties
  • Easy deletion when you quit

3. What is “too much”?

  • Location, diet and sleep reveal health conditions
  • Social data exposes your friends too

4. Who runs it?

  • Tech firm: ads and data sales
  • Hospital: HIPAA applies
  • Most health apps fall outside HIPAA
  • Government: hard to opt out
